**Job market paper:**
{: .section-title}

<div class="paper">
<div class="paper__title"><a href="https://mayaanorman.github.io/docs/blandm.pdf"><em>Develop or Conserve? How the U.S. Government Allocates Public Lands</em></a></div>
<div class="paper__old-title">Previously circulated as <em>Economic Tradeoffs and Institutional Guidance in Public Resource Allocation: Evidence from U.S. Public Lands</em></div>
<p class="paper__abstract">
How do bureaucrats translate national policy into local decisions? I study this question at the Bureau of Land Management (BLM), whose local offices decide where to restrict development — a source of billions in annual public revenue — to conserve natural resources. Drawing on offices' planning records, I provide evidence that these land-use decisions can be modeled as collections of discrete choices. I use this framework to guide AI agents in transforming 90,000 pages of records into a dataset suitable for discrete-choice econometric methods. Motivated by the two principal federal statutes governing offices’ decisions, I then estimate a model in which each office weighs the conservation value of a choice, net of revenue forgone, against the administrative cost of publicly justifying departures from established practices. I find substantial heterogeneity in how much revenue offices are willing to forgo for conservation, with suggestive evidence indicating that local preferences, rather than environmental benefits, explain this heterogeneity. I also find that offices are willing to sacrifice net resource benefits to avoid justifying departures from established practices. Counterfactuals show that this preference creates a powerful policy lever; by publicly recommending specific conservation practices, BLM headquarters can achieve conservation outcomes similar to those of a price-like instrument at comparable cost.
</p>
</div>

**Working papers:**
{: .section-title}

<div class="paper">
<div class="paper__title"><a href="https://mayaanorman.github.io/docs/bottlebills.pdf"><em>The Importance of Earnings from Recycling</em></a></div>
<p class="paper__abstract">
Recycling — collecting discarded items and redeeming their scrap value — channels income to marginalized populations. This paper evaluates the importance and prevalence of these earnings throughout the US. Specifically, this paper shows that positive shocks in recycling earnings increase household food expenditures and improve birth outcomes. Bottle bills — a policy requiring refundable deposits on beverage containers — boost food expenditures by approximately 10.2%, increasing recycling earnings by roughly $1400/year (in 2015 dollars) — an amount achievable by collecting 38 containers per day during the sample period. The effect is concentrated among low-income, able bodied households — a population likely to recycle for cash. Moreover, recycling earnings increases improve birth outcomes — a proxy for economic well-being in low-income populations. Back-of-the-envelope calculations imply that informal recycling markets are 50% as effective as food stamps at reducing the incidence of low birth weight. The striking comparability between recycling earnings and a targeted welfare program can be explained by both the size of recycling generated income and the distinct populations each income source reaches.
</p>
</div>

<div class="paper">
<div class="paper__title"><a href="https://mayaanorman.github.io/docs/greenparadox.pdf"><em>Empirical Tests of the Green Paradox for Climate Legislation</em></a></div>
<div class="paper__coauthor">with <a href="https://wolfram-schlenker.info/">Wolfram Schlenker</a></div>
<div class="paper__status">R&amp;R at <em><u>Journal of Public Economics</u></em></div>
<p class="paper__abstract">
The Green Paradox posits that fossil fuel markets respond to changing expectations about climate legislation, which limits future consumption, by shifting consumption to the present through lower present-day prices. We demonstrate that oil futures responded negatively to daily changes in the prediction market's expectations that the Waxman-Markey bill — the US climate bill discussed in 2009-2010 — would pass. This effect is consistent across various maturities as the proposed legislation would reset the entire price and consumption path, unlike temporary supply or demand shocks that phase out over time. The bill’s passage would have increased current global oil consumption by 2-4%. Furthermore, a strengthening of climate policy, as measured by monthly variations in media salience regarding climate policy over the last four decades, and two court rulings signaling limited future fossil fuel use, were associated with negative abnormal oil future returns. Taken together, our findings confirm that restricting future fossil fuel use will accelerate current-day consumption.
</p>
</div>

**Selected work in progress:**

<div class="paper">
<div class="paper__title"><em>Tracing the Formation and Origins of Standards in Environmental Review</em></div>
<div class="paper__coauthor">with <a href="https://simondgreenhill.github.io/">Simon Greenhill</a></div>
<p class="paper__abstract">
How do conservation standards emerge and spread across environmental impact statements prepared by public land agencies? When conducting environmental review, local agency offices must weigh the benefits and costs of alternative land uses and publicly justify their decisions. Offices frequently defend their choices by citing established conservation standards rather than relying solely on the benefits and costs specific to each decision, which can be more burdensome to justify to stakeholders. We use large language models to construct a novel dataset tracing the origins and diffusion of these standards. We investigate whether they spread through networks of influence among local offices or through the adoption of centrally issued guidance.
</p>
</div>

<div class="paper">
<div class="paper__title"><em>Weather Volatility, Food Waste and Firm Adaptation</em></div>
<div class="paper__coauthor">with <a href="https://www.ing.uc.cl/academicos-e-investigadores/roberto-alfredo-zuniga-valladares/">Roberto Zúñiga</a></div>
<p class="paper__abstract">
Weather volatility can make consumer demand harder to predict, potentially increasing food waste at restaurants and grocery stores. Higher temperatures may also accelerate food spoilage, further increasing waste. Large chains can mitigate these effects by investing in inventory management and demand forecasting software, while smaller businesses may lack the resources to do so. Using quarterly establishment-level data on organic and non-organic waste collected in New York City from 2016 to the present, this project examines how weather volatility and temperature affect food waste. We then investigate whether larger chains are better equipped to mitigate these effects, shedding light on how firms' capacity to adapt to environmental conditions shapes both economic losses and environmental outcomes.
</p>
</div>
